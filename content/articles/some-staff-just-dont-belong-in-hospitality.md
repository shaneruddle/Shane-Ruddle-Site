Some people are lovely, capable and hard-working, but they just aren't suited to a customer-facing job. In hospitality, first impressions count for a lot, and some people simply come across as unfriendly, even when they aren't.

## The waiter who looked miserable

I went out for dinner recently with my partner. The food was good and the service was good. But the man serving us looked miserable. His body language, the way he spoke, everything about him said he was unhappy to be at work.

If I wasn't in hospitality myself, I'd probably have walked away thinking the service was unfriendly. Instead, it made me think he just doesn't belong in that kind of role.

## We see it all the time

I'm not immune to this. It happens in my own restaurants too, and the first thing we have to work out is whether someone is just having a bad day, or whether that's simply the way they are.

We've had to let people go in the past for exactly this reason. They weren't bad staff. They got the orders right and spoke good English. But their manner came across as negative.

I remember one young woman at one of my venues in particular. Her English was really good, and she had the potential to be a great member of staff. But she came across as a little unfriendly, even though she really wasn't.

## Can it be taught?

That's what you have to work out as an owner or manager. Sometimes it can be coached out of someone. But with some people it can't. It's just their nature, and unfortunately, in those cases we normally let them go.

Scratch beneath the surface and they're often very nice people. They just don't come across that way, and in hospitality, the customer only sees the surface.

## The other side of it

It works the other way too. We have staff who regularly get orders wrong and turn up late. They're far from the perfect employee. But they come across as so friendly and welcoming that we keep them in the role, because that's what customers remember.

Mistakes and punctuality still matter, of course, and we work on them. But if I'm honest, a warm, welcoming first impression can matter more. Orders and timekeeping can be trained. A friendly nature usually can't. So when my teams hire, that's where a lot of the emphasis goes.

## My take

That's the whole point: some people just belong in a non-customer-facing role. It isn't a criticism of the person. Someone who looks unfriendly at a table might be brilliant in the kitchen, in the office, or in a job where first impressions matter less. Part of a manager's job is putting people where they'll shine.
