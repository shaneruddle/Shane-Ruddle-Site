One of the most annoying parts of owning businesses is paying for mistakes I didn't make. It happens all the time. Sometimes it's money out of my pocket, sometimes it's revenue we never see. Either way, the owner pays. And if I'm honest, I nearly always end up covering it. ## The deposit that went the wrong way

When one of my property agencies sells a condo, the buyer pays a 10% deposit. That takes the condo off the market and secures the deal. If the buyer pulls out later, they forfeit the deposit. Under our contract, and as is normal in the industry, half goes to the agency as commission and half goes to the seller for the inconvenience and the missed opportunity. We've still done our job, even if the buyer's circumstances change.

Recently a buyer pulled out. Instead of splitting the deposit 50/50, one of my managers kept only 5% of the deposit for us and gave the other 95% to the seller. We ended up with a few thousand baht instead of half.

In theory, we could have asked the seller to pay the difference back. That's easy for me to say, but I'm not the one who has to make that call. The deal had already fallen through, and the manager didn't feel comfortable phoning to say we'd made a mistake and wanted a lot of money back.

To be fair, he offered to pay for it himself. I declined. It was lost revenue rather than money going out, so it stung less. But in the end the seller won, the buyer lost, and so did we. That's business. More in [selling a condo in Pattaya](/articles/selling-a-condo-in-pattaya).

## The Mercedes and the pole

While I was away in China, I left my Mercedes with my car rental team to be serviced. One of the staff reversed it into a pole. The repair came to about ฿30,000.

He paid some of it, but I paid the lion's share. The car's value has gone down too, because it now has an accident on its record. I wasn't in the car. I wasn't even in the country.

I didn't claim on insurance, because the higher premiums would probably have cost about the same, and repairs take much longer through insurance. Either way, it was going to cost around ฿30,000.

The problem is simple. When someone earns ฿15,000 a month, ฿30,000 is two months' salary. I can't realistically charge them the full amount. The law limits it too. Under Thailand's Labour Protection Act, an employer can only deduct damages from wages if the damage was caused wilfully or through gross negligence, and only with the employee's written consent. Even then, each deduction is capped at 10% of that pay packet. I'm not a lawyer, so check with one before deducting anything.

In practice, that settles it. On ฿30,000 of damage and a ฿15,000 salary, the most I could take is about ฿1,500 a month, for 20 months. And there's nothing stopping the employee from leaving, and then I'd be chasing them for ฿1,500 a month. It's hardly worth it. With such a big gap between what staff earn and what owners earn, you almost feel obliged to pay it yourself. It's still very annoying. More in [managing Thai staff](/articles/managing-thai-staff).

## The ฿500 fine

Years ago, a member of staff without a driving licence was stopped by the police on his motorbike while running an errand for work. He was fined ฿500.

His argument was that if he hadn't been out working, he wouldn't have been stopped, so we should pay. My argument was that if he'd had a licence, he wouldn't have been fined.

I paid anyway. ฿500 isn't much to a business owner, but it's a lot to an employee, and it wasn't worth the friction.

## Why I nearly always pay

When you weigh it all up, the money matters far more to them than to me. If I make staff pay a meaningful share, resentment often sets in. Even when it's clearly their mistake, and even when I've paid most of it, their part still feels big to them. Maybe I'm a bit too nice. But I nearly always pay.

## My advice: fix it so it doesn't happen twice

The one thing I always do is make sure the same mistake doesn't happen again. After the deposit mix-up, the rule is now written down, so there's no ambiguity next time.

With something like a car accident, there's not much you can put in place. Asking for a small contribution makes people think twice about being careless. Beyond that, if you own a business with a lot of staff, accept that paying for other people's mistakes is part of the job.
