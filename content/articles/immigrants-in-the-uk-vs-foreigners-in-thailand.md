I was chatting with a former policeman from the UK recently, and we got onto how differently Britain and Thailand treat people who move there legally. In Thailand, foreigners get far fewer rights than locals. In the UK, it often feels like the opposite. My view is that the right answer is somewhere in between. To be clear, I'm talking about legal immigrants, not illegal immigration.

## Thailand protects its own

Thailand is very protective of its people and its sovereignty. Some jobs are reserved for Thais by law. In most service businesses, foreigners can't own more than 49% of a Thai company, unless they qualify for an exception such as Board of Investment promotion or the US Treaty of Amity. Foreigners generally can't own land. And there's very little in the way of social benefits for foreigners. I've lived here for 22 years and paid tax, yet my rights aren't much different from a tourist here for two weeks. There is a path to permanent residence and citizenship, but it's extremely difficult, and only a small quota of residence permits is granted each year. ## The UK goes further the other way

In the UK, legal immigrants can start and own businesses on the same terms as British nationals, and they get access to NHS healthcare. Once someone gets settled status, they're treated much like a citizen, and there's a clear path to British citizenship.

In many ways that's great. But in my view, and from what that former policeman told me, it sometimes goes a little too far.

## The other side of the argument

Plenty of people would disagree with me. Most UK visa holders pay a yearly Immigration Health Surcharge on top of their taxes to use the NHS, and most can't claim benefits until they settle. Settlement has usually taken five years, and in November 2026 the government confirmed plans to make ten years the standard.

Supporters of immigration also point out that research, such as the Migration Observatory's, finds the fiscal impact depends heavily on age, skills and earnings, with many working migrants paying in more than they take out. Immigrants fill jobs the economy depends on, from the NHS to farming. And some would argue Thailand's approach makes it harder to attract the skilled people and investment it needs.

## Somewhere in between

It would be easy for me to cherry-pick the rules that suit my own situation. But honestly, I do think it should be somewhere in between.

Countries absolutely have the right to protect their culture and their people. Around the world there's a clear shift towards governments putting their own citizens first, whether it's America, Japan, China, Vietnam or Thailand.

Where I land is simple. If someone genuinely benefits the community, they should be welcomed with open arms. If they're a drain on resources and don't contribute, then I think Thailand has it right.
