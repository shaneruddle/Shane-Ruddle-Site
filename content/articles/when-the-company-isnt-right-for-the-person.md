We talk a lot about staff not being the right fit for a company. But more often than not, in my experience, it's the other way round: the company isn't the right fit for the person.

## The agent I thought would stay

One example sticks with me. We hired an agent at East Coast Real Estate who was genuinely very good. She had good prospects, and I honestly thought she'd be a long-term employee.

She left after three or four months.

I'd made the decision to hire her, so I did the exit interview myself. She simply said the company wasn't right for her. She felt we weren't generating enough leads, and she needed a certain number per day. In my opinion, that number was way over the top, but that was what she expected.

And fair play to her, she went to work for a much bigger company.

## Leads are subjective

We did tell candidates up front what to expect on leads, and we still do. The problem is that lead quality is hard to track.

She was getting far fewer leads than she'd hoped for. Maybe she thought that was fine as long as the quality was high, and then decided the quality wasn't any better than she'd expected.

From the company's side, our argument is that leads are only as good as how you work them, at least to some degree. All of that is subjective and open to discussion. But we never got that far. She'd already decided, because she had another position lined up.

## A step up doesn't hurt

When someone leaves for what looks like a step down, it hurts a bit more. When they leave to take a step up, you can't really mind.

So there were no hard feelings, and there rarely are. The only time there are is when someone does something unethical on the way out, like taking our listings.

## When your face doesn't fit

Company culture plays a big part too, in the office and even more so in restaurants.

I saw it very clearly at a bar I used to run. There was a core group of about half a dozen staff who'd been there a very long time, and they were very cliquey. You could come in and be excellent at your job, but if your face didn't fit, you weren't going to last.

It wasn't management making people feel uncomfortable or unwelcome. It was their fellow staff. And in the restaurant business, that happens quite often.

Offices have their own version of this: office politics. Someone can like the company, the way it operates and the opportunities they can see, and still find the politics too much to bear. I think it happens in pretty much every office. I've written more about that in [office politics: why I stay out of it](/articles/office-politics-why-i-stay-out-of-it).

The point is that the people you work with can make or break whether you stay in a company.
