Over the years I've noticed something that sounds backwards: the people who interview brilliantly often turn out to be the worst employees. And the shy, quiet ones often turn out to be the best.

## Interviews take practice

Most people don't rehearse interviews. The only real way to get good at them is to have had a lot of them. And if you've had a lot of interviews, it usually means you've been through a lot of jobs.

So the candidate who walks in confident and polished, says all the right things and tells you exactly what they think you want to hear, may be good at interviews precisely because they don't stay anywhere long.

## The quiet ones

On the flip side, the people who come across as shy, reserved and a bit humble often end up being really good. They haven't had the practice, because they haven't needed it.

## What I do now

- **Use the CV as a reference point.** I keep it in front of me during the interview and ask about it: the gaps, the short stays, the reasons for leaving.
- **Hold at least two interviews** for senior roles or anything with real responsibility.
- **Get a second opinion.** Someone else in the room will pick up things I miss.

I've written more about this in [how I'd hire staff if I were starting again](/articles/how-id-hire-staff-if-i-were-starting-again) and [hiring and firing in Pattaya](/articles/hiring-and-firing-in-pattaya).

## My take

A great interview tells you someone is good at interviews. It doesn't tell you they'll be good at the job. Look past the performance, check the CV, and don't overlook the quiet candidate.
