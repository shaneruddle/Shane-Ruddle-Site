A lot of the advice about Thailand you'll find online, and sometimes hear in person, comes from people who have only been here a few years. Often it isn't even their own experience. It's something they heard and ran with. Before you take anyone's advice, it's worth asking two simple questions. ## The Britney Spears problem

When Britney Spears first came on the scene, she was singing songs about love, and some older artists asked what someone that young could really know about it.

That's what a lot of Thailand advice reminds me of. People with a few years here speak with the confidence of someone who's seen it all. And there's a second part to the comparison. A young pop star often isn't singing about her own experiences. A songwriter wrote the words. A lot of advice works the same way: secondhand, repeated as if it were first-hand.

## Some governments take it seriously

This isn't just an expat gripe. Bad online advice has become a big enough problem that some governments now regulate it. In China, influencers who post about medicine and health, law, finance or education are required to show platforms proof of qualifications, such as a degree, licence or professional certificate. The rules got wide attention in late 2025, although some of them build on livestreaming regulations from earlier years.

You don't have to agree with China's approach to see the point. Plenty of people take advice they find online at face value and run with it, without asking whether the person giving it has ever actually done the thing they're talking about.

## "You can't make money in Thailand"

A good example is the old line that you can't make money here. "If you want to make a million baht in Thailand, come with ten million." In other words, everyone loses money.

In my experience, that usually comes from people with little or no business experience here, or people who had one bad experience. Saying nobody can make money makes losing it feel less personal.

To be fair, if you'd asked me two or three years in, I might have said the same thing. It is hard. But 20 years on, I have made money here, and my experience tells me something different. More in [from bar manager to 7 businesses](/articles/bar-manager-to-7-businesses).

## Check one: how long have they been here?

The first question to ask is simply how long someone has lived here. If it's a few months or a year, take their advice with a pinch of salt.

"How long have you been here?" gets asked a lot in expat circles, and some people puff their chests out because they've been here longer. But there is some truth behind the question. Someone who has been here 20 years and has really become part of life here should naturally know more about how things work than someone who just arrived.

## Check two: have they actually done it?

Time alone isn't enough. Make sure their experience matches the advice.

If someone has been here 20 years but has never owned a business, don't take business advice from them. If they've never been in a relationship with a Thai partner, don't expect solid relationship advice. There's a good chance they're just passing on someone else's story, which may be distorted or one-sided.

## Bar chat and bitter stereotypes

A lot of the worst advice doesn't come from the internet at all. It comes from conversations in bars.

Say someone has had a bad experience with a girlfriend. He feels she was only with him for his money, and he's bitter about it. So he goes around the bars telling everyone that's what they're all like. He paints everyone with the same brush. Then someone new to the city hears it and carries that attitude into their own relationship.

Once you've lived here a reasonable amount of time, your view becomes more balanced, because you've seen things from a lot of different sides, not just one bitter one. More in [no, they didn't all meet in a bar](/articles/they-didnt-all-meet-in-a-bar).

## The complicated stuff is where it goes wrong

The same thing happens with property. Outside of condos, the rules on ownership are genuinely hard to understand. So are visas, driving licences, tax and anything to do with immigration or government offices. It's all complex, and it's very easy to get wrong. More in [nominee companies in Thailand](/articles/nominee-companies-in-thailand) and [Thai visas in Pattaya](/articles/thai-visas-in-pattaya).

## When long-timers won't say "I don't know"

Here's something I've probably been guilty of myself. When you've been here a long time and someone new asks you a question, you don't want to admit you don't know. You feel you should know, after all these years.

So instead of saying "that's not my area", or "the government changes these rules a lot, so I'm not sure what the latest is", some people just give an answer. Maybe they get it wrong, maybe they fill in the gaps. And the newcomer takes it as the truth, because it came from someone who has been here a long time.

## Even the professionals

I'd go further. Be a little sceptical even of so-called professionals. Rules here change often, and in my experience even immigration officers and lawyers sometimes don't know.

## My advice

It's the same advice you'd give anyone, anywhere. Look for someone with real experience in the specific thing you're asking about. Look for an expert, not an internet expert.

Don't take anything as fact, whether it comes from a bar, the internet or an office. Check it. Something that sounds solid can still turn out to be wrong. And if a decision could cost you money or a relationship, get information from as many people as you can before you make it.
