I've been in business in Thailand for around 20 years, and I'll be honest: I don't deal with my own tax at all. My accountants handle it, both for the companies and for me personally. That isn't because I'm lazy about it. It's because, as a foreigner, you realistically couldn't do it yourself.

## Why you can't do it yourself

Everything is in Thai. You're dealing with government agencies, and all the forms and filings are in Thai. Until recently it was all paper forms. Some filings can now be done online, which helps, but it's still all in Thai.

On top of that, accountancy is one of the professions foreigners generally aren't allowed to do here, apart from limited cases under agreements between Thailand and certain countries. So even if you wanted a foreign accountant to do the work, you'd struggle to find one who could.

If you run a small business here, you have to have a Thai accountant. There's no way around it.

## Don't expect tax advice

Here's the part people might not expect. Over the years I've worked with at least half a dozen accountants, partly because many of the businesses I bought came with their own. Not one of them has ever come to me and said, "If you do this, you can save yourself some tax."

In my experience, accountants here file your returns and government documents. That's it. They're reactive, not proactive.

Where they do step up is when the tax department comes knocking, which has happened in my businesses over the years. Then they get more involved, explaining what's gone wrong or why extra tax has to be paid.

## The taxes we actually pay

Across my businesses, these are the main taxes we pay. Rates are as they stand in late 2026 and can change:

- **Personal income tax** on my salary, deducted through the company I hold my work permit with. It's charged on a sliding scale, from 0% up to 35% at the top.
- **Corporate income tax** on company profits. The standard rate is 20%, with lower rates for smaller companies.
- **VAT** at 7%, once a business turns over more than 1.8 million baht a year.
- **Social security**, paid by both the employer and the employee, at 5% each of the employee's wage, up to a monthly cap.
- **Withholding tax.** This doesn't depend on the type of business, but on certain payments within it. When a company pays for things like services, rent or advertising, it holds back a small percentage and pays it to the Revenue Department.

We pay these too, and they're the ones business owners often don't think about, because the accountant just deals with them:

- **Withholding tax on dividends** when profits are paid out to shareholders
- **Land and building tax** on property
- **Signboard tax** on the signs outside your premises
- **Stamp duty** on certain documents, such as leases and contracts
- **Specific business tax** and transfer fees when property is sold

One that catches people out is property tax on a building you rent. When you lease premises, you're not just paying the rent. Normally the landlord will expect you to pay the annual land and building tax to the government as well, so make sure you factor it in. ## Getting advice in your own language

While accountants have to be Thai, foreigners can work as consultants and advise on tax. One of the accounting firms we use, BSS, is run by a Thai accountant, and her foreign husband acts as a consultant. It's useful to have someone you can talk to in your own language who can explain how tax works in Thailand.

## What I'd tell a new business owner

- **Get a Thai accountant from day one.** You'll need one for your company filings, and for your personal tax if you're on a work permit.
- **Know what you're paying for.** Expect someone who files your paperwork correctly and on time, not someone who will plan your tax for you.
- **If you want advice, find it separately.** Depending on the size of your business, it may be worth paying for a consultant who can explain things in your language.

*I'm not an accountant or tax adviser. This is my experience as a business owner, not tax advice.*

## My take

Tax in Thailand isn't something a foreigner can handle alone. Accept that you need a Thai accountant, understand what they will and won't do, and if you need proper advice, look for it elsewhere.
