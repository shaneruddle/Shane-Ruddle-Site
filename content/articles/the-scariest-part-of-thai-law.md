What worries foreigners in Thailand most right now isn't just the nominee law itself. It's that you can't be sure how it will be applied to you. You can believe you're doing everything correctly, and find out it's being interpreted differently, with consequences up to and including prison.

*I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice.*

## Even the lawyers were puzzled

In the recent Hua Hin nominee cases, heard on the same day, some defendants were given six-month sentences without suspension, and two were taken to prison. In another courtroom, a couple received suspended sentences and fines. According to local reporting, a lawyer involved in the cases was puzzled by the difference.

If the lawyers can't explain why similar cases ended so differently, it's no wonder people are panicking. (More on those cases in [the Hua Hin nominee cases](/articles/the-hua-hin-nominee-cases).)

## It used to go one way

Until recently, the general expectation was that if you were caught with a nominee structure, you'd be given suspended sentences and fines, and a chance to put things right. In an earlier Phuket case, for example, 23 people were convicted and given suspended sentences and fines, and the companies were ordered to be dissolved.

Hua Hin suggests you can't count on that any more.

## Why ambiguity is so dangerous

This is what I said to someone recently: you never really know how your own circumstances will be looked at, or how the rules will be interpreted.

A structure your lawyer set up years ago, which everyone treated as normal, can suddenly be seen in a completely different light. The rules on paper may not have changed much, but how strictly they're enforced, and how a particular court or official reads them, can change a great deal.

## The other side

To be fair, nominee arrangements have always been illegal. The authorities would argue that nobody should be surprised that the law is now being enforced, and that consistent outcomes will come as more cases go through the courts. Supporters of the crackdown see it as protecting Thai land ownership, which has long been the law's purpose.

## My advice

- **Don't assume you're fine because everyone else does it.** That was never a defence, and it certainly isn't now.
- **Get independent legal advice on your own situation**, from a lawyer who wasn't involved in setting it up.
- **Don't make sudden changes without advice.** Moving shareholders or transferring property in a panic can make things worse.
- **Keep records.** Being able to show where money came from, and who really paid for what, matters.
