Part of me loves getting involved in businesses I know nothing about. There's something exciting about learning something new, rather than buying another restaurant where I already know how it works, what can go wrong, and the mistakes I won't make again. It's good for diversification too. But it comes with obvious challenges, and I've learned that the hard way.

## London Pie

The first example that comes to mind is London Pie, our pie manufacturing company. It was food, so on paper it wasn't a big leap from restaurants. In reality, it was a completely different business: manufacturing, supply chains and selling to supermarkets.

That might be one of the reasons it didn't work.

## A handful of customers, not hundreds

The biggest difference was the customers. In a restaurant or at Pattaya Rent a Car, we have hundreds of customers. With London Pie, the whole business hinged on relationships with about half a dozen supermarkets, and supermarkets can be very difficult to deal with.

Each of those customers was hugely valuable, so you couldn't afford to lose one. In a restaurant, no single relationship carries that much weight, and there's far less pressure on you.

## When to step outside what you know

If you're good at business in general, you can take that into most businesses. Most, not all. You'll be able to get going with pretty much anything if you're business-minded.

The trouble is specialist businesses, where you need expertise you don't have. Then you're relying on other people.

So my advice is this: step outside your main area when there's some overlap, some crossover or synergy with what you already do. Don't go for something at the completely opposite end of the scale. If you're a restaurateur, don't suddenly go and buy a golf course.

## The grass isn't always greener

I gave this exact advice to someone recently. He had a very good core business that he'd run for 15 years, but he kept being tempted into other things.

I understand why, because I've done it myself. When you see other people doing well in their businesses, it's natural to wonder whether you're in the wrong line of work, and whether what they're doing is more lucrative. So you step into it, not realising they've been at it a long time and learned a lot along the way.

It's the grass is greener on the other side of the pitch. My advice to him was to double down on what he already has. He has a very good core business, and he'd be far better off concentrating on that than jumping into things that don't work.

## My take

Trying something new keeps business interesting, and it can spread your risk. But the further you stray from what you know, the more you depend on other people's expertise. Stretch yourself, just not too far in one go.
