I've brought up both my boys in Pattaya. I've already written about [whether Pattaya is good for families](/articles/is-pattaya-good-for-families) and about choosing a school. This one is about the day-to-day: what's genuinely great about being a dad here, and what's hard. For me, the hardest part hasn't actually arrived yet.

## The best bit: life is outside

Here, the weather doesn't dictate what you do. There are no freezing winters and no weeks stuck indoors. If the boys want to play football, go swimming or ride their bikes, they can, pretty much all year round.

The flip side is the heat. In the middle of the day it's sometimes simply too hot to do much, so you learn to plan around it: mornings, late afternoons and evenings.

## It doesn't cost a fortune

The second thing is cost. Compared with the West, doing things with your kids here is relatively inexpensive. Money doesn't really stop me or the boys doing anything we want to do. Days out, activities, sport, eating out together: none of it feels like a big decision the way it can back home.

## Holidays without leaving town

This is something we do quite often, and you probably wouldn't even consider it in the West: we just go and stay in a hotel. Sometimes it's a hotel right here in Pattaya.

A lot of the hotels here are built with families in mind. Many have water slides, small water parks and plenty of facilities for kids. Quite a few also run kids' clubs and organised activities. So it feels like you're on holiday, even though you're in the same city the boys were born in. It's a break for everyone, without the airport.

## The hard part now: no family to hand them to

One thing I'd warn any parent about is not having close family around.

When the boys were younger, I was lucky: their grandmother lived with us, and that was a huge help. It matters less now they're older. But I know my situation was unusual. Most people who come here with young children don't have that. And you can extend that to friends: many families arrive without a network of people they'd trust with their kids.

That means you're looking after your children yourself most of the time. There's no popping them round to grandma's for the afternoon or calling on a sister for the weekend. Nannies are available and affordable here, but a nanny isn't the same as a trusted family member. If you're moving with small children, be honest with yourself about how much support you'll actually have.

## The hardest part is still to come

I think the hardest part will come when the boys reach working age.

They were born here, they've grown up here, and Pattaya is home to them. But they only have British passports, so in Thailand they're foreigners. Being born here doesn't make you Thai unless a parent is Thai. A lot of jobs are reserved for Thai nationals. Practising law is one, and a long list of trades and manual jobs are closed to foreigners too. Other careers, like medicine, need Thai professional licences that are very hard for a foreigner to get.

So the country they think of as home may limit what they can do for a living, unless they start their own business, as I did, or build their careers elsewhere. I've written more about why I think Thailand should open up a path for children like mine in [Thailand should be welcoming families](/articles/thailand-should-be-welcoming-families).

> **Disclaimer:** The rules on which jobs foreigners can do, and on residency and citizenship, change. Check the current position with the Department of Employment or a qualified immigration lawyer before planning around them.

## The other side

The reserved occupations exist to protect Thai workers, and every country decides for itself who gets citizenship and the right to work. There are also routes, such as permanent residency and naturalisation, though they're slow and demanding. And plenty of children raised abroad end up working in another country anyway, by choice. Growing up here may even give the boys an advantage wherever they go.

## My take

I wouldn't swap it. My boys have had an outdoor childhood, with more experiences than I could have given them back home, at a fraction of the cost. But go in with your eyes open. Make sure you'll have support when the children are small, and think early about what they'll do when they grow up.
