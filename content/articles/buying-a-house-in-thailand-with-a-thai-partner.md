Here's the short version. If you're a foreigner buying a house in Thailand with a Thai girlfriend or wife, the land will legally belong to her, not you, and marriage doesn't change that. You can protect yourself, but only if you set it up properly at the Land Office on the day you buy, not later.

I've lived in Thailand for over 20 years and I run two real estate agencies here, and I'll be honest: this is still one of the most confusing areas of Thai property law. If it confuses people in the industry, it's no surprise buyers get it wrong.

*I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. Get an independent lawyer, one who isn't connected to the seller or the agent, before you hand over any money.*

## The basic rule: foreigners can't own land

A foreigner can own a condo outright in their own name, as long as the building's foreign quota (49% of the floor area) isn't full. Land is different. Foreigners can't own it, full stop.

So when a foreigner and his Thai partner buy a house, the land goes in her name. In most cases he's put up most or all of the money, and on paper he owns nothing.

## Getting married doesn't fix it

You'd think marriage would help. It doesn't, and in one way it makes things worse.

When a Thai woman with a foreign husband buys land, the Land Office usually asks the husband to sign a declaration that the money is her own personal money, not shared marital money. So the land is legally hers alone, even if he paid every baht. If the marriage ends, he can't count on getting half of it.

## How foreigners protect themselves

These are the legal options, and all of them need to be registered on the title deed at the Land Office. A private agreement signed at home isn't enough.

- **A 30-year lease.** You lease the land from your partner for up to 30 years. If she sells, the buyer takes the land with your lease still attached. Promises to renew after 30 years often aren't enforceable. (More in [is a 30-year lease safe in Thailand?](/questions/is-a-30-year-lease-safe-in-thailand))
- **A usufruct.** This gives you the right to live on the property and take any income from it, and it can last for your whole life. The catch is that it ends when you die, and you can't sell it or leave it to anyone.
- **A superficies.** This lets you own the house itself while she owns the land underneath it. Put the building permit in your own name as well.
- **A registered mortgage.** Register a mortgage against the land for the money you put in, so she can't sell it or borrow against it without dealing with you first.
- **A prenup and wills.** If you're marrying, a prenup has to be registered at the same time as the marriage. Wills on both sides make it clear what happens if one of you dies.

Most people choose either a lease or a usufruct, usually alongside a superficies for the house.

## What about setting up a company?

For years, the standard approach was to set up a Thai company to own the land, with the foreigner holding 49% and Thais holding 51%. Plenty of people still do it.

The problem is that the Thai shareholders have to be genuine. If the foreigner paid for their shares, they're nominees, and that's illegal. Being married to her doesn't change the test. What matters is whose money paid for the 51%. The Land Office can ask Thai shareholders to prove where their money came from, and the authorities have been cracking down. (More in [nominee companies in Thailand](/articles/nominee-companies-in-thailand).)

If the company was set up just to get round the land rules, I wouldn't do it.

## The other options

- **Buy a condo instead.** It's the one way to own property outright in your own name.
- **Put it in your children's names.** Thai children can own land, but any sale or mortgage of land owned by a minor needs court approval.
- **The 40 million baht route.** Investing at least 40 million baht in approved investments can let a foreigner own up to one rai of land for a home, with ministry approval. Very few people use it.

## The other side

It's easy to call this unfair, especially if you've put up 15 million baht for a family home. But Thailand isn't unusual. Plenty of countries limit foreign land ownership to stop land prices being pushed out of reach of local people. It's also worth remembering that the Thai partner takes a risk too: with a 30-year lease in place, she owns land she can't use or sell freely.

## My advice

Decide how you're going to protect yourself before you buy, not after. Register it at the Land Office on the same day as the transfer. Use your own lawyer. And only put in money you could live with losing if things went wrong.
