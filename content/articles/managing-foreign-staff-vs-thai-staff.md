I employ both foreign and Thai staff across my businesses. Neither is better. They're different, and knowing the differences makes you a better boss.

## Communication

The obvious difference is communication. Foreign staff understand me and the direction I want to go, and they often understand foreign customers more easily, because of the language and the culture.

Thai staff are better with Thai customers, and they're very good at creating a good atmosphere, whether it's an office or a restaurant. They see work in a more light-hearted way, and it shows.

## What motivates each

In my experience, most foreign staff are motivated mainly by staying in Thailand. Anyone who's been here a while knows how hard it is to get a job, so once they have the chance, they want to hold on to the life it gives them. Few I've met are driven by money or are especially ambitious. Most just want enough to live here.

Thai staff are motivated a little by money, but mostly by job security. They have one advantage foreigners don't: if a Thai wants work, they can find it. I often see Thai staff leave a job one day and start a new one the next. More in [finding a job in Pattaya](/articles/finding-a-job-in-pattaya).

## Giving feedback

With foreign staff, I'm more direct, maybe even a bit harsh, because there's no language barrier and it suits the culture. With Thai staff, I'm more careful about what I say and more constructive. More in [managing Thai staff](/articles/managing-thai-staff).

## Where the friction comes from

I've seen a lot of friction between foreign and Thai staff, usually when the foreigner doesn't understand how things work here or how Thai staff like to be treated.

It's a tough spot. The foreign manager is often the one accountable for results, caught between an owner who wants a profit and staff who want a friendly atmosphere. Those don't always go hand in hand. The conflict I see most is when a foreign manager is too friendly and easygoing with the Thai staff, then struggles to discipline them because they've lost their respect. More in [what makes a good manager in Pattaya](/articles/what-makes-a-good-manager-in-pattaya).

## Who stays longer?

It's not clear-cut. Hemingways Jomtien has been open eight years and has both. I've only had two managers there: the first for two or three years until we parted ways during the pandemic, and Robert, who's been with me about five years. Some of the Thai staff have been there since day one, but with far more Thai staff, more have also come and gone. At Pattaya Rent a Car the team is all Thai, and some have been with me for over ten years.

## Foreign or Thai manager?

It depends on the business. In a restaurant with foreign customers, I think you need a foreign manager, someone people come in to chat with. Even a Thai with excellent English can't really relate to customers talking about, say, the latest US midterms.

If customers aren't spending hours building a relationship with the place, I'd lean towards a Thai manager. They're cheaper to hire and cheaper to let go if it doesn't work out, and a really good one is extremely loyal. That's not me being unfair, it's how the market works. The minimum wage in Chonburi is ฿400 a day, roughly ฿10,000 to ฿12,000 a month, while a foreigner's work permit generally requires a salary of at least ฿50,000. You can pay either more, of course, but foreign staff generally cost much more.

So it comes down to the type of business and your appetite for salaries. More in [hiring foreign staff in Pattaya](/articles/hiring-foreign-staff-in-pattaya) and [why good managers are hard to find](/articles/why-good-managers-are-hard-to-find-in-pattaya).
