Pattaya is the cheapest of the usual places Westerners retire to, with a lot more to do and a much bigger community. Money is usually not the problem once you adjust your lifestyle. Culture shock is. I'm not retired, but after 20 years running businesses here I've met a lot of people who came to Pattaya to retire. Here's what I've seen, and the practical side: what it costs, which visa, healthcare and where to live.

## Retiring in Pattaya at a glance

| What | The short answer (2026) |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly cost, single | About ฿50,000 to ฿60,000 living comfortably |
| Monthly cost, couple | About ฿70,000 to ฿80,000 |
| Visa | Retirement visa: aged 50+, ฿800,000 in a Thai bank or ฿65,000 a month |

## Why do retirees choose Thailand?

The cost of living, the lifestyle, the weather, and how welcoming the locals are.

## Why Pattaya in particular?

Of the usual places where lots of Westerners retire, Pattaya is the cheapest. There's also a lot more to do, a much bigger community, and a far more mixed crowd of people.

Many retirees here are men, and for some of them Pattaya is a chance to start again, including finding a new relationship.

## Is money the biggest problem?

Usually not, and it's [rarely why people leave](/articles/do-retirees-leave-pattaya). Some people arrive thinking they haven't quite got enough. But once you adjust your lifestyle, so you're not out drinking, partying or eating in the best restaurants all the time, it becomes very affordable. For real numbers, read [Is Pattaya Expensive?](/articles/is-pattaya-expensive)

## How much do you need to retire in Pattaya?

For a single person living comfortably and going out about once a week, I'd say ฿50,000 to ฿60,000 a month, and ฿70,000 to ฿80,000 for a couple. A one-bed condo is around ฿18,000 a month, and eating out is part of the culture here, costing about what cooking at home would in the UK.

The biggest mistake I see is assuming it's cheaper than it is and overspending in the first few months. Put your own numbers into my [cost of living calculator](/cost-of-living-calculator), and read [how much money you need to retire in Pattaya](/questions/how-much-money-do-i-need-to-retire-in-pattaya).

## Which visa do retirees use?

Most retirees use the retirement visa. You need to be 50 or over and show ฿800,000 in a Thai bank, an income of ฿65,000 a month, or a mix of the two, then renew it every year at the immigration office in Jomtien. As Thai visas go, it's relatively simple, but I'd still use an agent.

My [retirement visa page](/thai-visas/retirement-visa) compares the Non-O, O-A and O-X, and has a checker that tells you which money route fits your pension and savings. Once you're here, you'll also have [the 90-day report and TM30](/articles/tm30-and-90-day-report-pattaya) to keep on top of.

## Do you need health insurance?

Yes. The care here is first class, and we've always used Bangkok Hospital Pattaya, but uninsured patients who can't pay can be turned away by private hospitals and sent to a government one. Get insurance before you need it, and check your limits, because a big claim can skyrocket quickly. Insurance gets harder and more expensive to buy the older you are, so don't leave it until you're 70. More in [healthcare in Pattaya](/articles/healthcare-in-pattaya).

## Where do retirees live in Pattaya?

If I were picking for a retired couple, I'd choose Jomtien. It's quieter, you're right by the sea, and there are lots of retired couples, so it's easy to make friends. The buildings are older too, so ฿20,000 a month usually gets you a bigger one-bed than in Pratumnak or Wongamat. If Jomtien still feels busy after a few years, the natural next step is East Pattaya.

Rent first, ideally for a few years, before you buy anything. See my guide to [living in Jomtien](/areas/jomtien) and the [best area to live in Pattaya](/articles/best-area-to-live-in-pattaya).

## What catches retirees out?

Culture shock. And not just retirees: tourists and anyone who comes here assume things work the same way as back home.

Take the law. We get people from abroad who feel they've been treated unfairly over something small and want to get lawyers involved. In practice, small grievances rarely get anywhere here, and nobody is going to take up a minor complaint for you. That takes some getting used to. (More in [the cultural mistakes foreigners make](/articles/cultural-mistakes-in-thailand).)

The pace is another one. Here it's very much "tomorrow, tomorrow". In the West, things get done on time because that means customers and business. It's very different here, and it takes a while to get your head around.

## My best advice for retirees

Try to [learn some of the language](/articles/learning-thai). You don't need to be fluent. Basic Thai won't earn you special respect or different treatment, but it tells people around you that you're not a tourist. You've chosen Thailand and settled here. It just makes living here that little bit easier.

Then make the effort to [make friends](/articles/making-friends-in-pattaya): golf, padel, the gym and running clubs are full of people who were new once too.

For the visa side, read [visas in Pattaya](/articles/thai-visas-in-pattaya), and if you're moving from Britain, [moving to Thailand from the UK](/move-to-thailand/from-the-uk). If you'll be driving, sort out an [International Driving Permit](/articles/international-driving-permit-thailand) before you come.
