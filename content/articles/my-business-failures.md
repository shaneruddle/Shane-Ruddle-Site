On paper, I probably look successful. I run several businesses in Pattaya and I've been doing it for over 20 years. But that's only half the story. Along the way I've had my fair share of failures, and I've learned more from them than from anything that went right. Here they are, along with why I think each one didn't work.

## Fairways Golf Range: the nets

The honest truth is I went into Fairways because I loved golf, not with a proper business head. I didn't do my numbers, and selling trays of balls for 35 baht was never going to cover the rent on that much land. I've written the full story in [my biggest business failure](/articles/my-biggest-business-failure).

But what finally closed it was the nets. When we took it over, we re-netted the whole range for well over a million baht, on the understanding they'd last a long time. Three years later they were already falling apart. We had to re-net it again, for over a million baht again, and this time go higher, because the old nets weren't high enough. For a business that was already struggling, that was the final nail in the coffin.

**Lesson:** a business that's barely getting by can't survive one big bill.

## Birdie: the golf website

Birdie was a free golf handicapping website, and the plan was to make money by selling green fees to the golfers using it. The golfers were there. The golf courses weren't interested in moving from paper vouchers to digital, the office staff I dealt with had no reason to change, and I could never reach the owners. So I carried on driving paper vouchers around until COVID finished it off. The full story is in [Birdie: the golf website that ran into paper vouchers](/articles/birdie-the-golf-website-that-ran-into-paper-vouchers).

**Lesson:** if the people you depend on have no reason to change, a good idea isn't enough.

## The Golf Club bar: a location that changed

When The Golf Club opened, it was in a pretty good location. Over time, though, every business around it became an entertainment venue, and we were the only one that wasn't. Entertainment venues generally make more money than restaurants, so the rents went up. Our core customers stopped coming, the traffic made it harder and harder just to get there, and a lot of our customer base moved out of Pattaya. In the end the place just didn't suit the location any more. (More in [selling The Golf Club](/articles/selling-the-golf-club).)

**Lesson:** a good location today isn't guaranteed to be one tomorrow.

## London Pie: margins too thin

The numbers simply didn't work. Margins were thin, and we had to sell an awful lot of pies to pay for the staff, the production and the rent. We were at the mercy of the big supermarkets like Tesco, Makro and Foodland, who squeezed those margins even further.

To make it work with a single product, you'd realistically need to sell tens of thousands of pies. It was a chicken-and-egg situation. If we'd spent millions of baht on the right equipment we might have won the big contracts, but we couldn't win the contracts until we'd spent the millions.

**Lesson:** one product on thin margins needs huge volume, and huge volume needs huge investment.

## BizPay: established competition

BizPay was a barter network, competing with Bartercard. I was the franchisee for Pattaya. There were lots of reasons it didn't work, but the main one was competition. Bartercard is an internationally recognised barter system, and building a rival network from scratch was always going to be a struggle.

**Lesson:** taking on an established brand needs something much better, not just something similar.

## The Outback: COVID, then the roadworks

The Outback is still running today, so the business itself didn't fail. My ownership of it just didn't work out the way I'd hoped.

I bought it just before COVID. That's a reminder that outside factors can make or break any acquisition, however well you plan. Then, as soon as we came out of COVID, they started resurfacing Siam Country Club Road. That doesn't sound so bad, but while they worked on the stretch outside, nobody could actually reach the restaurant. We went months with customers unable to get to us.

**Lesson:** you can't control everything, so make sure you can survive what you can't control.

## The cloud kitchen

I started a delivery-only cloud kitchen during COVID, and on paper it was perfect. In practice, the delivery apps' commission of around 30% left razor-thin margins, and the kitchen was too far from central Pattaya, so Grab barely showed us to customers. When COVID ended, deliveries dropped off and it fizzled out. The full story is in [my worst business ever: the cloud kitchen](/articles/my-worst-business-ever-the-cloud-kitchen).

**Lesson:** a great idea and a great business aren't the same thing.

## Hemingways Lakeside: still a tough one

Lakeside hasn't failed, but it's been a difficult one, and it shows that even after 20 years I still get things wrong. There's strong competition, and I think we were a bit early. The catchment area has a limited number of customers. Everyone says the area is up and coming, and I agree, but it isn't happening fast enough for the number of places already there.

**Lesson:** being early can hurt as much as being late.

## My take

Every one of these taught me something I still use today. If you're starting out, don't be put off by the idea of failing. Do your numbers, keep some money back for the big bill you didn't see coming, and keep an eye on how things are changing around you.
