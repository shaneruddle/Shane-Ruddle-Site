My rule for haggling in Thailand is simple: if the price isn't printed or clearly displayed, you can haggle. But after 20 years here, I hardly ever do.

## Where haggling is fine

Taxis, market stalls, anywhere the price isn't on show for everyone to see. That's where a bit of negotiation is normal and expected.

## Why I stopped

When I first came to Thailand, haggling was part of the experience. The longer I've stayed, the less I want to do it.

Partly I just can't be bothered with the hassle. But mostly, something doesn't feel quite right about pushing the price down with someone who probably doesn't earn much.

You walk away feeling like you've won because you got something down from ฿250 to ฿200. Then you realise you've saved yourself about a pound. That means nothing to me, and an awful lot to the person selling it.

If you enjoy it, there's a middle way: haggle for the experience, then pay the going rate anyway once you've "won". You get the fun of the back-and-forth, and the seller doesn't lose out.

## Know what things should cost

If a price looks inflated, it's fair to question it. With the internet and AI, you can get a pretty good idea of what something should cost before you buy. (Here's my take on [dual pricing in Thailand](/articles/dual-pricing-in-thailand).)

## Prices are on everything now

I was walking around Chatuchak Market in Bangkok recently and noticed that most things have a price on them now. There aren't many stalls left where the price isn't displayed. It's very different from what I remember when I first came here, 15 or 20 years ago.

So haggling matters less than it used to. And when you do get the chance, it's worth asking whether the saving really means more to you than it does to them.
