For most of my 20-plus years in Thailand, nobody really looked at nominee companies. In 2026 that changed. In September the Department of Business Development, working with the Department of Lands, the police, the DSI and the Anti-Money Laundering Office, began screening more than 125,000 companies that hold land or condos. My view is that people already in one shouldn't panic, but anyone arriving now shouldn't start one. More in [foreign ownership, 99-year leases and the condo quota](/articles/foreign-ownership-99-year-leases-and-condo-quota) and [the biggest mistakes property buyers make in Pattaya](/articles/biggest-mistakes-property-buyers-make-in-pattaya).

*I'm not a lawyer, and this is my opinion from running property agencies here, not legal advice. Speak to an independent lawyer about your own situation.*

## What a nominee company is

A Thai company that owns land has to be majority Thai owned. In a nominee company, that majority is held by one or more Thais who are shareholders in name only. In my experience, there are three tell-tale signs:

- They didn't invest any money.
- They have no say in how the company is run.
- They get no benefit from it.

Using Thais as nominees to get around the foreign ownership rules is illegal, under both the Foreign Business Act and the Land Code. Under the Foreign Business Act, acting as or using a nominee can mean up to three years in prison and fines of ฿100,000 to ฿1,000,000.

## Why so many foreigners used them

Mostly it's about land. Foreigners can't own land in Thailand, so for years the common route was to buy a house through a company. The foreigner held 49%, and Thai shareholders held the rest on paper.

It happened with businesses too. Someone without a Thai partner would open a bar or restaurant and put staff, acquaintances or a friend's girlfriend down as shareholders. It's the same principle, and the same three signs apply.

## The crackdown

For a long, long time, there was no real crackdown. Over the last year, the authorities have started looking into companies to work out whether the Thai shareholders are genuine or nominees, including checking the financial trail behind them.

## If you already own a house this way

My advice is to sit tight. There's a lot of fear-mongering, and some of it comes from lawyers, because it's business for them.

As far as I can see, the authorities aren't turning up and taking people's homes. It's an investigation process. As I understand it, you get a warning first, and if they conclude the company was set up to buy the property, the worst case is that you're ordered to sell it within a set period. The Land Code does allow the authorities to order land held unlawfully for a foreigner to be disposed of.

If you live in the house and don't plan to sell, I'd sit tight and see how it plays out. But be realistic: prices for company-owned houses are going down, because demand is drying up and selling is getting harder. If you've got cold feet and want to protect the value, that's a reason to act sooner.

## Businesses seem to be left alone, for now

Actively trading companies appear to be largely left alone at the moment. I assume that's because even if they were set up incorrectly, they're still paying tax, employing Thais and giving a fair amount back. That could change, so don't rely on it.

## If you're arriving now

**Buying a house:** I'd rent. Until there's more clarity, there isn't a good legal way for a foreigner to own a house and its land outright. My suggestion is to keep your property back home, rent it out, and use that income to rent here. People say rent is wasted money, but it isn't when someone else is paying rent on a home you legally own. More in [renting a condo in Pattaya](/articles/renting-a-condo-in-pattaya).

If you do want to own, the legal routes are a condo in your own name within the foreign quota, or a long lease on a house and its land. More in [buying a condo in Pattaya](/articles/buying-a-condo-in-pattaya).

**Opening a business:** at the very least, find Thai partners you actually know and trust, and set it up properly. There are also legal routes that allow more foreign ownership, such as BOI promotion, a Foreign Business Licence, or the Treaty of Amity for US citizens. More in [setting up a business in Pattaya](/articles/setting-up-a-business-in-pattaya).

Some people lend their Thai partners the money for their shares. My view is that investigators look at where the Thai shareholders' money came from fairly late in the process, and that it would be hard to prove the loan was made to get around the law. But it is one of the things they check, and lending money for that purpose is illegal, so I wouldn't set anything up on that basis without a lawyer.

## The other side

Supporters of the crackdown say the rules exist to keep Thai land in Thai hands, and that nominee structures have pushed up prices and skirted the law for decades. Lawyers point out that "nobody has lost their house yet" isn't a legal defence, and that penalties can include fines and prison. Both are fair points, and why anyone in this position should get proper advice.
