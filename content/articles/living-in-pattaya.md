Living in Pattaya is slower, sunnier and friendlier than life in the West, and as of 2026 a single person can live comfortably on about ฿50,000 to ฿60,000 a month. I've lived here for over 20 years. If a friend from the UK asked me what it's really like, I'd tell them about the pace of life, the weather and the people, and then about the things nobody mentions until you arrive.

## What's the best thing about living in Pattaya?

The pace of life. Everything here is slower and more easygoing than in the West, and it doesn't feel like a grind.

It takes some getting used to. When I first came here at 23, the pace was honestly a bit too slow for me. The longer you stay, the more you go with the flow, and now I wouldn't swap it.

Thais call it sabai sabai, roughly "relaxed, no rush". In practice, a job that would take a couple of days at home can take a week here. In the UK you'd simply move on to a competitor, but most local businesses work at the same pace, so you adjust. My trick is to start three things at once, knowing each will take a week.

Then there's the weather. It's sunny and over 30 degrees all year. Even now, in the rainy season, it's still sunny and hot most of the time.

And the people. The vast majority of Thais are genuinely friendly and accommodating, to foreigners and to each other. Like anywhere, you'll meet the odd exception, but they're rare.

## What do newcomers find hardest?

Oddly, the same weather that draws them here. There are no real seasons, so it's hot every single day. At home, the cold forces you to get up and get moving. Here, the constant heat can leave you feeling a little less energised, and it feeds that slower pace. It's the attraction, and it's also the thing people struggle with most.

## How much does it cost to live in Pattaya?

As of 2026, I'd say a single person can live comfortably on about ฿50,000 to ฿60,000 a month, and a couple on ฿70,000 to ฿80,000. That assumes you live much as you would at home, going out once a week rather than every night. For a fuller breakdown, see [is Pattaya expensive?](/articles/is-pattaya-expensive)

Children change the sums. Most foreign families choose international schools, mainly for the English-language teaching and a curriculum that carries over if they move home. They're private, so they're not cheap. They still cost less than in the West, but fees will take a big bite out of a budget. I've written separately about [what Pattaya is like for families](/articles/is-pattaya-good-for-families), including school fees.

Eating out is part of the culture here, and it isn't expensive. Eating out most days costs roughly what cooking at home would in the UK.

## What's healthcare like?

Excellent. I normally use Bangkok Hospital Pattaya, the biggest hospital in the city, and the service is second to none.

A couple of years ago, my son needed treatment while we were in the UK, and we spent most of the day waiting to see a doctor. Here, you'd be in and out in an hour. In the UK the treatment was free and here you pay, but given the choice, I'd pay every time. That's not a dig at the NHS; the private hospitals here are simply very good, and the doctors are well qualified. In all my years here, I can't remember getting bad advice.

You'll hear stories about big bills, mostly from people used to never seeing the cost. At home you pay through your taxes; here you pay at the counter. It's the same thing, wrapped differently.

## Do you need health insurance in Pattaya?

Yes, absolutely. Most newcomers catch a stomach bug or a bout of food poisoning in their first few months. You get used to it, but you will get ill at some point.

The roads are the other reason. They can be precarious, and minor accidents happen. People who'd never think about insurance in the UK, where treatment is free, find it hard to get their heads around, but here it just makes sense.

## Is it easy to make friends in Pattaya?

Very. Whatever you're into, whether that's golf, padel, tennis or the gym, you'll find your people. Everyone here has living in Pattaya in common, and everybody has a story, so there's always something to talk about.

It's not a lonely place, either. Even if you're slow to make friends with other foreigners, Thais are friendly and always happy to chat.

## How do you get around?

You can manage perfectly well with motorbikes and taxis. Bikes are cheap to rent, buy, insure and fuel, and plenty of people get around on them. My advice: ride confidently and stay alert, because the roads can be dangerous. If you're driving, read [what I tell every Pattaya Rent a Car customer](/articles/driving-in-pattaya).

A car is more comfortable and a lot safer, for a bit more money. That's part of why I've [run a car rental company here since 2010](/articles/16-years-of-pattaya-rent-a-car). Traffic isn't bad; it only really builds up on the long holiday weekends in high season.

## What's the biggest mistake people make?

Assuming it's cheaper than it is. Pattaya is good value if you manage your money, but most people spend far more than they expected in their first few months. Eventually they settle into a routine, go out less and budget better, but I see a lot of money wasted early on.

## What I'd tell a friend before moving here

- **Rent before you buy.** I wouldn't buy property anywhere in the world without trying the place first, ideally for a few years. Here's my guide to [which area suits you](/articles/best-area-to-live-in-pattaya), and [what to know before buying a condo](/articles/buying-a-condo-in-pattaya).
- **Expect a 12-month lease.** Most landlords want a 12-month contract and two months up front, which you lose if you leave early. Shorter rentals exist, but they're harder to find.
- **Budget realistically,** especially for the first few months.
- **Get health insurance** before you need it.
- **Go with the flow.** Things take longer, so plan around it.

## Why I'm still here

Honestly, after more than 20 years, part of it is that I've been here too long to start again at home. I love England, but going back would be hard now.

If I left tomorrow, I'd miss the pace of life, the people, my friends, Thai and foreign, and the weather. Most of all I'd miss the lifestyle and what I've been able to build here.
