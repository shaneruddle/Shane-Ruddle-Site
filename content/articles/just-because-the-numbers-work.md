One of the biggest mistakes business people make, me included, is assuming that if the economics make sense, the business will work. In Thailand especially, I've seen plenty of ideas that were perfect on paper and went nowhere, and a few I could do tomorrow but choose not to.

## Birdie and the paper vouchers

I built a free golf handicapping website and planned to make money selling green fees through it. Going digital would have saved the golf courses money and sold more rounds. But they'd always used paper vouchers, the office staff I dealt with had no reason to change, and I could never reach the owners. The full story is in [Birdie: the golf website that ran into paper vouchers](/articles/birdie-the-golf-website-that-ran-into-paper-vouchers).

## Golf course kiosks

Out on the course, most Thai golf courses have kiosks selling drinks, crisps and golf balls, each with a member of staff. Nothing there needs cooking or handling, and in a lot of the world it would just be a vending machine. Financially, it makes perfect sense.

But the courses have always done it this way, and they have relationships with the people who run the kiosks. I first thought about this 15 years ago, and it still hasn't happened.

## Ordering from your table

Digital ordering in restaurants is convenient for customers and saves on staff. The numbers work. But you lose the human side of being served, and I believe, like plenty of others, that it's part of the experience of eating out. Save a member of staff, and you might lose some of what makes people come back. (More in [why I won't put digital ordering screens in my restaurants](/articles/why-i-wont-put-digital-screens-in-my-restaurants).)

## The car rental I could automate tomorrow

When I rented a car in the UK, I saw one person at reception. Everything else was digital.

I could do that at Pattaya Rent a Car. Picture it: a customer walks in, picks a car on a touch screen, pays by PromptPay, and gets a code for a lockbox. The screen tells them which car is theirs, and off they go. I could definitely cut staff that way.

But I won't. A lot of our customers come back because they know our staff by face and by name. That relationship is worth more than the wages I'd save.

## The lesson

Before you chase an idea because the numbers stack up, ask:

- **Who has to change for this to work, and do they have any reason to?**
- **Who actually makes the decision, and can you reach them?**
- **What would you lose that doesn't show up on a spreadsheet?**

The economics are only half the story. People, habits and relationships are the other half.
