For 20 years I thought that when I paid a customer for one of my staff's mistakes, I was making a goodwill gesture. It turns out I wasn't. In Thailand, the business is legally responsible. I only realised this recently, so I'm writing it down for anyone else running a business here. More in [paying for my staff's mistakes](/articles/paying-for-my-staffs-mistakes).

*I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. If you're facing a claim, speak to a Thai lawyer.*

## The business is on the hook

Under Section 425 of Thailand's Civil and Commercial Code, an employer is jointly liable with an employee for damage the employee wrongfully causes to someone else in the course of their work.

In plain English: if one of my staff messes up while doing their job and a customer loses out, the customer can come after the company. It doesn't matter that I wasn't there, didn't know, or would never have done it that way.

## When it applies

Roughly two things have to be true:

1. **The employee did something wrongful.** Under Section 420, that means they wilfully or negligently caused damage to someone's property, body or rights. An honest misunderstanding where nobody lost anything may not count.
2. **It happened in the course of their job.** A rental clerk processing a booking, an agent handling a deal, a waiter serving a table. If they were off doing something purely personal, it's a different story.

## Can I claim it back?

Section 426 says an employer who has compensated someone for an employee's wrongful act is entitled to reimbursement from that employee.

In practice, it's not that simple:

- **Wage deductions are limited.** Under the Labour Protection Act you can only deduct for damage the employee caused wilfully or through gross negligence, with their written consent, and no more than 10% of any pay packet.
- **Beyond that, it's a separate claim.** If the amount is bigger, you'd have to pursue the employee yourself, and if they've left the company that can be hard.

## What it's changed for me

The biggest thing is realising there are more risks to doing business, especially in Thailand, than I'd appreciated.

It's quite scary when you think about it. One employee can walk into your business and do damage big enough to take the company down. I always knew, in theory, that people's actions affect the company. But when it's a lot of money and your name is on the business, it really hits home.

The timing has made it hit home even more. On 12 November, Nvidia's CEO, Jensen Huang, resigned after reports about his extramarital affairs, and the company's share price fell. He's the CEO, so it's a little different from a member of staff, but the principle is the same: one person's actions can hurt the whole company. Seeing that in the news alongside what's happened in my own business is what made it click.

It's also shown me how important standard operating procedures are. If a member of staff goes against a written procedure, it's much easier to show they went rogue and acted against our policies. If nothing is written down, and staff haven't signed to say they understand the procedures, that's very hard to prove.

So my takeaway is simple: we need to be more professional and get things written down. More in [how I run seven businesses](/articles/how-i-run-seven-businesses).

## The other side

Some owners will say this is unfair: why should the business pay for something it didn't do? The answer the law gives is that the business chose the employee, benefits from their work, and is better placed to carry the risk than the customer. Employees, for their part, are protected from having their wages stripped for every mistake. It's a balance, and after thinking about it, I think it's a fair one.
