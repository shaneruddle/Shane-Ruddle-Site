There's a lot of advice out there about Thailand, and I've written plenty of it myself. But I've come to believe something that might sound odd from someone who gives advice for a living: sometimes the best thing you can do for a newcomer is say less. Part of the fun of a new country is not knowing.

## The friend who told him everything

Years ago, a friend of a friend from back home turned up in Pattaya. On his first night, my friend started giving him the full lowdown on exactly how everything works here, nightlife included.

I remember telling him to tone it down. That newcomer was going to work it all out soon enough. But half the fun of those first few weeks is not knowing: believing the place is exactly as magical as it looks. Take that away on day one and you've taken away something he'll never get back.

## My own first trip

I remember sitting in my family home in the UK before my very first trip to Thailand. My older sister, who'd travelled a bit, was in the room, and she told me I wasn't ready. Thailand probably wasn't the best place for a first-ever backpacking trip, she said.

I half agreed with her. But my attitude was: I'll just see what happens. She said I couldn't just go over there and see what happens; I needed to give it more thought than that.

I didn't. I had almost no expectations. I didn't really know anything about the place, other than that there were Thai people there. And that was what made it so good. Everything was new, and nothing could disappoint me because I hadn't built anything up. More than 20 years later, I'm still here.

## The best moments aren't in the guidebook

It's great to arrive in a country with everything planned. But often the best experiences and the most fun come when you don't know what's going on or where you're going.

You get lost because you didn't ask the right questions. Then something lovely happens: a local gives you a lift in the back of their pickup, or you end up somewhere you'd never have found on purpose. Those are the stories you tell for years. Nobody tells stories about the itinerary that went to plan.

## Where I'd draw the line

That doesn't mean saying nothing. There are three things I'd always tell a newcomer, because they're not mistakes you want to discover for yourself:

- **The law.** Some Thai laws aren't common sense to a visitor and are unique to Thailand. Vaping is an example: e-cigarettes are illegal here, and people do get fined. These aren't things you want to learn the hard way.
- **Real financial risk.** If someone's about to put serious money into a property, a business or a relationship, I'll speak up. A small overcharge is part of the learning; losing your savings isn't. (More in [scams in Pattaya](/articles/scams-in-pattaya).)
- **Health and safety.** Helmets, the roads, the sea, the heat. Some lessons are too costly to learn first-hand.

Beyond that, I'd let people find their own way.

## The other side

There's a fair argument the other way. Plenty of people have had a miserable or expensive first trip because nobody warned them, and a bit of preparation can stop a small mistake turning into a big one. Not everyone enjoys uncertainty either. Some people relax only when they know what's coming, and that's fine too.

## My take

Tell people what could genuinely hurt them: the law, their money and their safety. Then step back. Let them get a bit lost, get a few things wrong and find out what's really going on in their own time. That's part of the adventure, and it's how they'll end up loving the place.
