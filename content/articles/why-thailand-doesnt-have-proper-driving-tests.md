In the UK, you learn to drive and then you get your licence. In Thailand, you get your licence and then you learn to drive. That one difference explains a lot of what you see on the roads here.

## Earning a licence in the UK

When I learned to drive in the UK, I paid for lessons with a qualified instructor, sat a theory test, and failed my practical test the first time before passing on the second. Nobody was handing out licences. You had to earn one.

## Getting a licence in Thailand

Here, it feels like the opposite mindset. The system seems to want you to pass.

When I got my Thai licence, there was a reaction test, which is frankly a bit pathetic, and a depth-perception check where you line up two sticks. There wasn't even a theory test back then. There's a multiple-choice theory test now, but from what I hear a lot of the questions aren't very logical.

I clearly remember an older man doing the tests alongside me. He failed both the reaction test and the distance check, but they just let him keep going. With the sticks, I think he was guessing, and when he eventually guessed right, they waved him through to the next stage.

## Why it's so easy

I think there's a reason for it. In the UK, if you don't have a licence, it's not the end of the world. You can get the bus or the train to work.

In Thailand, especially out in the villages and rural areas, if you can't ride a motorbike or drive a car, you've got very few options. Buses don't come along very often. I think the government is reluctant to make getting a licence any harder, because if a big chunk of people couldn't drive, the whole economy would grind to a halt.

## It shows on the roads

You can see the results every day.

**The Dolphin Roundabout.** Every time they've opened it up as a full roundabout, it seems to have been closed again soon after because of accidents. Nobody seems to understand how a roundabout works. Even now, with half of it shut off, people have no idea which lane they should be in.

**Tailgating.** It's everywhere, even on the motorway. Keeping a safe distance is common sense, but it's clearly never been taught.

**Unroadworthy vehicles.** That's not the test's fault as such, but it's another sign of how inexperienced a lot of drivers are.

The World Health Organization has ranked Thailand's roads among the deadliest in Asia for years.

## What I'd change

The lessons. Thailand needs proper driving schools and qualified instructors, and a provisional licence with L plates, so other road users know the driver is still learning.

It doesn't need to reinvent anything. Western countries have much safer roads, so there must be something in their approach. Take it and use it here.

## The other side

To be fair, Thailand has tightened things a little over the years, adding a theory test and training for new drivers, and licensed driving schools do exist. And for millions of people, especially in rural areas, a licence is how they get to work, so there's a real cost to making it harder. Lessons cost money, and not everyone can afford them. Any change would need to make proper training affordable, not just make the test harder.

## My take

If you're renting a car or a motorbike here, drive as if the people around you were never properly taught, because a lot of them weren't. Leave plenty of space, take roundabouts slowly, and expect the unexpected.
