After 20 years of managing people in Pattaya, and hiring managers of my own, I think two things separate good managers from bad ones here: adaptability and a level head.

## Adaptability

You can arrive with your own idea of how a business should work, but you need to adapt quickly once you discover how things really work here, with Thai staff, the regulations and everything else.

When I [left the Sportsman](/articles/bar-manager-to-7-businesses), the manager who took over came to me a few months in, pulling his hair out. He couldn't get through to the staff, he didn't understand the dynamic, and he thought a lot of them didn't like him. I told him the same thing had happened to me. Thai staff take time to warm up to a new foreign manager. Over the years they've worked with a lot of foreigners, good, bad and indifferent, so they're guarded. A few months later we sat down again, and he said things were much better.

Depending on your personality, expect it to take about six months.

## Be friendly, but earn respect

Thai staff can be playful, and it's tempting to think that mirroring that will win them over. It won't. They might be friendlier, but they won't respect you. They're not stupid. They know how things work.

What matters most to them is job security. They enjoy a bit of fun at work, but they don't expect it from the manager. They expect the manager to allow it, to drive the business, to make their jobs easier and, above all, to keep them employed. The way to do that is to improve the business and bring in customers. Do that and you'll earn their respect. More in [managing Thai staff](/articles/managing-thai-staff).

## Keep a level head

I've made this mistake myself, and I see other managers make it: letting your emotions run you. Thais don't like confrontation, especially in public, and if it happens they'll leave quickly. It's hard to stay calm when the kitchen has made a clear mistake and you're the one getting the earful from the customer. But if you walk in with a cool head and make your point calmly, you gain a lot of respect. Lose your temper and you lose their respect, and they won't want to work for someone when they don't know when it'll happen again. Go really over the top and you might as well throw in the towel.

## The time I lost it

A customer at Pattaya Rent a Car once rang to say he'd left a large prototype, important to his business, in the back of the SUV he'd rented. By then we'd rented the car out again, and my maintenance guy insisted there was nothing in it. It went back and forth, the customer got more and more irate, and I got more and more stressed. Eventually we reached the new renter, I drove out myself, and sure enough, it was in the back.

When I got back to the office I lost my temper with the staff, both for not taking it seriously and for cleaning the car without spotting something that size. It was only words, but I regretted it straight away. My manager, who'd worked with me a long time, calmed me down and, I assume, smoothed things over with the staff afterwards. My ego wouldn't let me apologise.

I can understand the temptation to go over the top. With a child who runs across the road, it makes sense: you're showing how serious it is. But very little at work is life and death. Something that feels hugely important to you often isn't that important to your staff, so an outburst doesn't land the same way. It just costs you respect.

## My advice

Be adaptable, give the staff time to trust you, earn their respect by growing the business, and keep a level head, even when it's hard.
