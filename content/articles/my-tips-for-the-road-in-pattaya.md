I've driven in Pattaya for 20 years and run a car and bike rental business since 2010. These are the rules I follow every time I'm on the road. For the basics, read [driving in Pattaya](/articles/driving-in-pattaya) and [scooter or car in Pattaya](/articles/scooter-or-car-in-pattaya).

## 1. Drive slowly

In the West, roads are kept well apart from people. Here, everything happens right at the roadside: dogs asleep, people selling food, businesses that almost spill onto the road. As I drive today, there's a baby in a pushchair being pushed along the edge of the road.

People step out without warning, because jaywalking rules, if they exist, are ignored. Dogs run out. You're in a dangerous machine very close to vulnerable people and animals. Most people learn to slow down eventually. My advice is to do it from day one.

## 2. Stay alert

It's easy to get distracted here: billboards, businesses trying to catch your eye, people at the roadside. Plenty of drivers and riders use their phones on the move. It's against the law, but it isn't really enforced. Drive with due care and attention at all times.

## 3. Keep your head on a swivel

Check your mirrors constantly. Motorbikes, cyclists and even runners come up the inside.

And don't make assumptions. You might have used a one-way street ten times, but don't assume nothing will come the wrong way. Bikes and cyclists often do. Even stepping onto the pavement, there might be a motorbike riding along it. Whether you're walking, riding or driving, people come at you from every direction, and it's very unpredictable.

## 4. Be careful late at night

I don't drive much late at night any more. After 10 or 11pm it really is the Wild West. Many traffic lights switch to flashing amber, which means they're effectively off, and junctions become a free-for-all. I think it's meant to keep traffic flowing when the roads are quiet, but it brings its own dangers.

Drink-driving is much better than it used to be, but late at night I'd argue a lot of people on the road have been drinking. Be doubly careful.

## 5. Don't trust zebra crossings

I see people nearly get clipped on zebra crossings every day, by motorbikes and sometimes cars. Drivers here often don't stop for them, so don't assume they work like they do in the West. A crossing gives you a better chance of getting across safely, but it doesn't give you the right to step straight out. Look both ways and check what's coming. You're not protected.

## 6. The roads don't always make sense

The way some roads are set up causes confusion, hesitation and accidents. To take a U-turn on Sukhumvit, you have to speed up to get into the fast lane, then slow down to turn. On the junction towards the Royal Cliff, the layout makes you think you have right of way when you don't, and the road markings are so worn that nobody really knows. I only know because I've used it for 20 years. There's another junction near Sukhumvit Soi 87 that's just as bad. I'd say there's an accident there every week, and nothing has changed. A friend who lives right next to it still isn't sure who has right of way.

So slow down and avoid sudden decisions, but try not to hesitate either. Drive with confidence.

## 7. Remember who else is on the road

Pattaya's roads are a mixing pot. Some drivers are used to driving on the other side of the road. I once watched a customer pull out of Pattaya Rent a Car on the wrong side and crash within a minute of renting the car, right outside our office. Driving cultures differ too, and some people drive fast and aggressively. With few speed cameras around the city, they can get away with it for a long time. Expect the unexpected.

## 8. Always wear a helmet

Lots of people here ride without a helmet. That doesn't mean you should. Buy your own, but not an expensive one: if it doesn't fit under the seat, there's a chance it'll be taken. Helmet theft is petty, but it happens.

## 9. If you have an accident

There are police boxes on many junctions. You rarely see officers directing traffic, and I think they're mostly watching the CCTV, but they're on hand to mediate if there's an accident.

The first priority is medical help. The good thing about everything happening out in the street is that you're rarely waiting alone. Motorbike taxi drivers and passers-by will stop to help, and the volunteer rescue crews, like the Sawang Boriboon foundation, are usually first on the scene. If you call the police or an ambulance, they come quickly.

For a minor accident, people often agree something at the roadside and go on their way. If it's more serious, wait for the police and medical help.
