If you buy a business, change the concept, bring in new staff and it fails years later, that's on you, not the person who sold it to you. It sounds obvious. But in my experience, when things go wrong, a lot of people look for someone to blame, and the seller is an easy target, especially if the seller is doing well.

## The buyer who blamed me for years

I've told the story of selling The Golf Club before, in [selling The Golf Club: how a coincidence made me the bad guy](/articles/selling-the-golf-club). This is the part I keep coming back to.

I advertised the business online, and the first person to contact me offered close to my asking price. Once he took over, he changed the concept from a bar and restaurant into more of a girly bar. Honestly, I thought that was probably the right call.

I was completely transparent. I gave him all the information, and it was all correct. The business wasn't making money at the time, because it was just coming out of COVID, and he knew that. He also knew he was going to change the concept anyway, so the old numbers mattered less to him.

A few months in, the landlady sold the building and the new owner put the rent up, which was pure coincidence. But he had no experience of running a business, let alone a business in Thailand. After two or three years, it failed. He sold it on and got some of his money back, but he definitely lost money.

And for years afterwards, he blamed me. An apology did eventually come, but it took a long time.

## Why people blame the seller

I can understand it. When you've lost money, it's easier to believe you were sold a bad business than to accept that your own decisions didn't work out. And if the person who sold it to you seems to be doing fine, it's easy to resent them.

But look at what changed. A different concept, different staff, a different owner running it day to day, years after I'd handed over the keys. I honestly can't see how the outcome could be the seller's fault.

## Due diligence is the buyer's job

If a seller deliberately deceives you, that's a different story. But if a seller simply doesn't volunteer everything, it's your job to dig.

Do your own research. Go through the accounts, the lease, the licences and the staff. Talk to the landlord and the neighbouring businesses. Sit in the business at different times of day and count the customers. Don't rely on the seller, the agent or anyone else to do it for you.

And accept that some things can't be foreseen. Anything we discussed before the sale, he knew about. Anything that came up six months later, neither of us could have predicted. You can't blame the person who sold you a business for something that happens long after you've taken it over.

*This isn't legal advice. If you're buying a business in Thailand, get an independent lawyer to check the lease and the paperwork.*

## Take responsibility for your own decisions

If you're buying a business, do your homework before you sign: the numbers, the lease, the location, the staff. Once it's yours, every decision after that is yours too, especially if you change what made the business work in the first place.

And if you're selling, be honest, hand over properly, and then accept that what happens next is out of your hands. Some buyers will blame you anyway. That's part of selling a business.
