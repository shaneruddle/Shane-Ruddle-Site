Thailand has some unusual rules on selling alcohol, and visitors often get caught out. As someone who runs several restaurants and bars, here's how they actually work out on the ground. For what it's worth, I haven't had a drink myself in about 12 years, so I have no personal axe to grind.

## The afternoon ban has finally gone

For decades, alcohol couldn't be sold between 2pm and 5pm. That ban was lifted on a trial basis at the end of 2025, and since May 2026 the normal selling hours have been 11am to midnight.

Good riddance. In practice it mostly hit shops like 7-Eleven, and we still had customers in the afternoon, but I always thought it was draconian. I believe it was brought in years ago to stop people drinking in the afternoon. I don't know many other countries with a rule like that.

## Holiday and election bans

These hurt more, and they're still here. Sales are banned on major Buddhist holidays and on election days, and they often fall at weekends. Usually it's 24 hours, from 6pm to 6pm the next day, though sometimes it runs longer.

It does hit revenue, but not as hard as you'd think. In Jomtien, the bars that only serve alcohol all close, because there's no point opening. People have fewer options, so we actually get more customers, because we serve food and soft drinks. The average spend is lower, but the drop in takings isn't as big as you might expect. ## If you're visiting

Check the dates before you travel. But honestly, it's 24 hours, and most people only drink for two or three hours of that anyway. Do something different that day, or if a drink really is a must, buy some the day before and have it in your hotel or condo.

## The pandemic changed how people see it

Before COVID, people used to get really upset about these bans. You'd see plenty of angry posts online about adults having their choices taken away. Then we went through months and months of closures. Since then, a 24-hour ban barely registers. People have softened, because it's pretty insignificant. ## Different drinking cultures

Part of the tension is that drinking habits differ. In the UK, drinking is just a way of life. People often aren't drinking to get drunk; it's habit, something we've always done.
