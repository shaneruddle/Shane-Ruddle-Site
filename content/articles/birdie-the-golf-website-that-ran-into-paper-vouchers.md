I once built a free golf handicapping website called Birdie, and the plan to make money from it was simple: sell green fees to the golfers already using it. It should have worked. What stopped it wasn't the golfers. It was golf courses that didn't want to move away from paper vouchers.

## What Birdie was

Birdie was a free handicapping system for golfers. The idea was that people would keep coming back to check their handicap, the traffic would build, and then we'd find a way to make money from it. I had a business partner on it at the time.

About a year in, it was going well. We had lots of repeat visitors, and valuable ones too. Golfers are exactly the kind of audience businesses want to reach.

## Selling green fees

The obvious way to make money was green fees. I was already selling golf vouchers on the side: I'd buy, say, 100 green-fee vouchers from a golf club at a discount, mark them up and sell them individually to golfers I knew. Doing that through Birdie seemed like a natural next step.

## The paper problem

The vouchers were physical paper. Every time stock ran low, I had to drive out to the course to collect more, and with thin margins, those trips made less and less sense.

Selling them was just as bad. Someone could buy a single green fee online, but then we had to physically deliver a paper voucher to them, which cost time and money.

The answer seemed obvious: go digital. Golfers buy online and get an email with a code to show at the course. It's how hotels and most bookings have worked for years. The courses would sell more, wouldn't need to print vouchers, and it would be easier for everyone. I thought everybody won.

## Why the courses said no

The golf courses just weren't interested. They'd always done it with paper vouchers, and digital was an unknown. Nothing was forcing them to change, so they didn't.

The bigger problem was who I was dealing with. Mostly it was office staff, who had no reason to care about saving money or being more efficient. Occasionally I'd speak to a manager, who wasn't that bothered either. The only person who might really have cared was the owner, and with big golf courses, the chance of ever speaking to the owner was slim.

## Carrying on the hard way

In the end, I played ball and carried on with paper vouchers. It meant a lot of running around to sell a couple of green fees at a time.

COVID finished it off. When the courses closed and the tourists disappeared, I was left holding a stack of green-fee vouchers I couldn't sell.

## The lesson

A good idea isn't enough if the people you depend on have no reason to change. Before you build a business around someone else's process, find out who actually makes the decisions, and whether they'll ever care about what you're offering. If you can't reach the person who benefits, you're probably selling to the wrong person.
