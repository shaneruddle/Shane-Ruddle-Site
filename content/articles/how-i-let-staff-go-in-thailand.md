Letting someone go in Thailand isn't that different from the UK on paper. If someone has worked for you for less than 120 days, which normally covers the probation period, there's no severance, although you still have to give proper notice or pay in lieu. After that, severance depends on how long they've worked for you, starting at 30 days' pay and rising to 400 days' pay for 20 years or more. What is different is how it usually plays out, and that's down to saving face.

## A warning is often enough

In my experience, once you give a member of staff a verbal warning, and follow it up with a written one, that's normally enough for them to leave on their own.

Unlike in the West, if Thai staff feel they don't belong in your company, or they know they're not doing the job, they'll usually leave graciously rather than wait to be sacked. Nobody loses face, and everyone moves on.

## Early on: it's just not the right fit

Most of the time, if someone isn't working out, it's very early in their employment. It normally means they're not capable of doing the job, and my managers and I can't see a real future for them in the company.

So the conversation is tactful and careful. We simply explain that we don't think the role is right for them. It's usually relaxed and casual.

That makes sense here, because a lot of young Thais move around and try different roles to see which one suits them. Not every job is going to fit, and that's fine.

## Gross misconduct: straightforward

If it's something serious, like gross misconduct, it's easy. You explain that they've broken the rules, that Thai law allows you to let them go for it, and that you're choosing to do so. In serious cases set out in the law, such as dishonesty or a serious breach of the work rules, no severance is due.

## Long-serving staff: we work with them

If someone has been with us a long time and starts underperforming, we don't let them go. We work with them. If they've been with us for years, it means they can do the job.

In more than 20 years, I can't think of a single time I've sacked someone who'd been with me a long time. It's either been early on, gross misconduct, or they've left of their own accord.

## Property: the one exception

The property businesses are slightly different from the restaurants and Pattaya Rent a Car, because our agents have targets. They also have a very flexible working environment, so targets are really the only way we can keep them accountable.

If an agent keeps missing their targets, we go through the process: a verbal warning, then a written warning, and then they'd be asked to leave. But again, once they're in that process, they often leave of their own accord. We very rarely have to have that final exit conversation.

## The other side

To be clear, we never put pressure on people or push them out to avoid paying severance. That's not what it's about. As soon as someone senses they don't belong, they often leave. Warnings should be about genuinely helping someone improve or deciding whether the role fits, not a way to push people out to avoid paying severance. A resignation that isn't truly voluntary can still lead to a claim at the labour court.

*I'm not a lawyer. This is my experience as an employer, not legal advice. Check the current severance rules with your accountant or a lawyer before letting anyone go.*

## My take

Handle it early, handle it kindly, and let people keep their dignity. Most of the time, they'll make the decision for you.
